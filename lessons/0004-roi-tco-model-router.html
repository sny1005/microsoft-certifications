What ROI has to be before it's anything else

Microsoft's bar for an AI ROI claim is four adjectives, and the exam uses them as a filter: ROI must be measurable, repeatable, aligned to business outcomes, and grounded in real usage analytics. A savings figure that fails any one of the four is a guess wearing a suit. This is why the discipline starts with a "no-AI" baseline: frame one process, set its boundaries and a baseline period, and capture baseline metrics — volume, average handle time, rework rate, backlog, satisfaction — before anything is built. No baseline, no ROI; only vibes. [source]

Five benefit categories. Learn them as a checklist you run against any proposed agent — a claim that fits none of the five isn't a benefit: [source]

CategoryWhat it capturesExample measures
Productivity gainsTime returned to workersTime saved per task, less manual entry, faster resolution
Cost savingsWork removed from the systemLabor hours avoided, ticket volume deflected
Revenue impactTop-line movementConversion rate, lead qualification, retention, upsell
Risk reductionBad outcomes made rarerCompliance violations avoided, error rates down
Strategic / innovation valueOptionality and capabilityNew offerings, faster experimentation — real, but hardest to monetize

Underneath the categories sit three value levers that actually generate the numbers: time saved per transaction, quality lift (error reduction, first-contact resolution, fewer escalations), and capacity/throughput. The six-step framework that organizes the whole exercise: define business outcomes → identify ROI drivers → calculate TCO → quantify benefits → compare (payback period, NPV, cost-benefit ratio, annualized ROI) → validate with stakeholders — Finance, Ops, process owners, and the AI CoE. Skipping the validation step is how a beautiful spreadsheet dies in a budget meeting. [source]

The Savings capability is the exam-relevant detail. On the agent's Analytics page you configure a savings value — per run or per tool — and Copilot Studio multiplies it across measured usage to produce a running savings figure. Two governance nuances: admins can disable money-based savings, in which case you track time saved and convert it to money yourself; and the configured per-run value is an assumption you must defend, which is why it should come from your own pilot telemetry rather than a vendor's brochure. The five TCO drivers

Total cost of ownership for an AI solution decomposes into five drivers. Memorize the set — AB-100 loves "which driver dominates this scenario" questions: [source]

  1. Infrastructure — compute, hosting, networking, environments.
  2. Development & integration — agent design and testing, prompt engineering, wiring into systems of record.
  3. Data quality & preparation — cleaning, deduplication, restructuring, permission repair so grounding works. The most underestimated driver.
  4. Expertise & staffing — scarce AI/ML and data skills, hired or grown.
  5. Operations & licensing — licenses, monitoring, evaluation, support, ongoing prompt and content maintenance.

Two structural points. First, TCO spans the full lifecycle, not just go-live: development (data prep, prompt engineering, agent design/testing, integration), deployment (infrastructure, licensing, security/compliance, API usage), operational (monitoring and evaluation, retraining, prompt library maintenance, support, training), change management (skilling, communications, process redesign), and eventually decommissioning. Second, the drivers connect backward to lesson 2: a polluted, over-permissioned estate is not just a grounding defect — it is a TCO line item, paid in the data-quality driver whether you budgeted for it or not. [source]

The ROI math — learn it until it's boring

Three formulas carry the exam's computational questions: [source]

Annual_Benefit = (Minutes_Saved_per_Run / 60) × Runs_per_Year × Labor_Rate  (plus error cost avoided and backlog/working-capital effects, when you can quantify them)

Net_Benefit = Annual_Benefit − Annual_TCO    ROI% = (Net_Benefit / Annual_TCO) × 100

Payback(months) = Initial_OneTime_Cost / Net_Monthly_Benefit

Worked example: the email triage agent

An agent triages inbound email. It saves 1.8 minutes per message; volume is 50,000 messages per month; expected adoption is 60%; loaded labor rate is $45/hour. Year-1 TCO is $300,000. Walk the arithmetic — slowly, because each step is a place the exam plants a distractor:

  1. Effective runs per year. 50,000 messages/month × 12 months × 60% adoption = 360,000 runs. The subtlety the exam loves: adoption multiplies the volume, not the minutes. If you skip the 60%, you overstate the benefit by two-thirds.
  2. Hours saved. 360,000 × 1.8 minutes ÷ 60 = 10,800 hours.
  3. Annual benefit. 10,800 × $45 = $486,000.
  4. Net benefit. $486,000 − $300,000 = $186,000.
  5. ROI. $186,000 ÷ $300,000 × 100 = 62%.
  6. Payback. Steady-state monthly benefit is $486,000 ÷ 12 = $40,500. Treating the $300k year-1 investment as the upfront cost to recover: $300,000 ÷ $40,500 ≈ 7.4 months.

Three traps to internalize: dividing minutes by 60 before multiplying by runs (order doesn't change the result, but forgetting the ÷60 entirely inflates benefits 60× — an instant sanity check); computing ROI on gross benefit ($486k ÷ $300k = 162%) instead of net; and reporting payback against gross monthly benefit while quietly ignoring run costs in years 2+. [source]

Sensitivity bands — because every input is a guess

Adoption, minutes saved, and labor rates are estimates, so a single-point ROI is false precision. Present three bands — optimistic, expected, conservative — varying adoption rate, minutes saved, labor rates, and rework effects. Holding the triage example's other inputs constant and flexing adoption alone:

BandAdoptionEffective runs/yrAnnual benefitNet (vs $300k TCO)ROI
Conservative45%270,000$364,500$64,500~22%
Expected60%360,000$486,000$186,000~62%
Optimistic75%450,000$607,500$307,500~103%

Notice what the band tells the decision-maker: even the conservative case clears zero — this investment is robust to an adoption miss. If the conservative band went negative, that's a pilot-shaped answer, not a build-shaped one. And on inputs: your own telemetry sits in the driver's seat; field research is a directional anchor at best. A measured 1.8 minutes from your pilot beats an industry paper's average, every time. [source, usage-analytics research]

Finally, the deliverable shape: the executive one-slide — problem and scope, the intervention, measured impact, financials, confidence and risks, and the pilot-to-scale decision. If your ROI case can't survive compression to one slide, it isn't ready for the room. [source]

Build, buy, or extend

The fourth rung of the Plan domain's judgment set. Each option has decision triggers — and characteristic risks the exam will dress up as scenario symptoms: [source, source]

OptionChoose when (triggers)Watch out for
BuildCompetitive differentiation is the point; prebuilt fails regulatory/compliance demands; data sensitivity requires full control; the org has strong AI/ML capabilityUpfront cost, long timeline, maintenance burden you now own
BuyTime-to-value is the priority; the process is standardized; AI maturity is low; you need vendor support and updatesLock-in, limited extensibility, feature gaps
ExtendYou want the Copilot/platform to absorb internal logic; domain knowledge arrives via grounding, connectors, plugins; the base model is good but needs enterprise adaptation — the balanced middleBalanced cost, faster than build, more flexible than buy — but see the data-prep row below

TCO by domain — the table with the punchline

TCO driverBuildBuyExtend
InfrastructureHighLowMedium
Development & integrationHighLowMedium
Data quality & preparationHighLowHigh
Expertise & staffingHighLowMedium
Operations & licensingHighMediumMedium

Everything in the table trends the way intuition says — build high, buy low, extend medium — except one cell: extend carries HIGH data-prep cost. The mechanism is lesson 2: extending means grounding a platform on your content through connectors and knowledge sources, and your content's duplicates, stale versions, and broken permissions don't clean themselves because you chose the cheap platform. "Extend is the middle-cost option" is true for four drivers and false for the one that kills projects. [source]

The nine-step analysis flow

  1. Define requirements — functional and non-functional.
  2. Assess strategic importance — differentiation or commodity? This single answer tilts the whole analysis toward build or toward buy.
  3. Assess vendor solutions against the requirements.
  4. Evaluate extend feasibility — can grounding, connectors, and plugins close the gap?
  5. Evaluate custom feasibility — skills, data, timeline.
  6. Estimate TCO across all five drivers, for each surviving option.
  7. Forecast ROI per option, with sensitivity bands.
  8. Weighted scoring across the criteria that matter to this org.
  9. Choose optimal value-to-cost — not lowest cost, not highest capability.

Note the ordering discipline: strategy (step 2) precedes vendor assessment (step 3), and both precede any costing. Teams that start at step 6 are pricing a decision they haven't made. [source]

The model router — one endpoint, many models

Azure AI Foundry's model router puts a single unified endpoint in front of multiple deployed models and routes each request intelligently — by task type, model capabilities, cost, latency, or custom rules — while giving you centralized governance: versioning, monitoring, analytics, and safety in one place instead of scattered per deployment. The motivations map directly onto this lesson's economics: cost optimization (an SLM handles the simple requests, the expensive LLM only when needed, fewer tokens burned overall), performance (latency-sensitive requests to fast models, specialized tasks to specialists), reliability (automatic failover, clean switching to new model versions), and hybrid strategies mixing all of the above. [source]

The four routing rule types — one scenario each

Rule typeWhat it doesConcrete scenario
StaticHard-coded mapping from a request attribute to a modelTask = classification → route to the SLM; domain = finance → route to the fine-tuned model
WeightedSplits traffic by percentage across modelsSend 10% of production traffic to the newly deployed model for A/B comparison, then ramp gradually — no hard cutover
FallbackRedirects to a backup when the primary failsPrimary model deployment errors or is unavailable → requests flow to the backup model automatically
Version-basedRoutes by model versionProduction traffic to the latest stable version; a canary cohort to the experimental version

The implementation sequence: define the routing strategy (cost-optimized, performance-optimized, accuracy-first, or hybrid) → register the models → configure the rules → integrate (agents, Copilot extensions, backends, multi-agent systems) → monitor latency, cost, accuracy, and usage distribution — then tune the rules to push cost down while holding performance. Two architect-level notes. Rules are per organization, not per textbook: run a routing-rule design workshop rather than copying defaults. And weighted rules plus monitoring are what make model upgrades boring — which is exactly what you want model upgrades to be. [source]

Model-choice economics: when a custom model or SLM is justified

The router arbitrages cost per request; this section arbitrates it per capability. The discipline from the strategy module: custom models are the last resort of a checklist, not the first instinct of a team. A custom model is justified only when prebuilt and fine-tuned catalog options demonstrably can't solve the problem — and the burden of proof runs through a specific sequence: prompt engineering → retrieval tuning → fine-tuning → and only then a custom build. "Off-the-shelf accuracy is insufficient" is a claim you must be able to show, as persistent low precision/recall after those cheaper interventions, in a domain where wrong answers are expensive or behavior must be near-deterministic. [source]

Catalog / prebuilt models suffice when…Custom model (or customized SLM) is justified when…
General-purpose tasks: summarization, classification, rewriting, translation, extractionDeep domain specificity — regulatory, contract, medical/legal/financial reasoning
Moderate accuracy requirements, standard enterprise knowledge interactionInsufficient off-the-shelf accuracy after prompt engineering, retrieval tuning, and fine-tuning
Time-to-value, low cost, low risk are the prioritiesHigh-volume economics — latency and cost per request at scale dominate
Standard compliance posture is acceptableSovereignty/residency or governance demands full control of training data, guardrails, explainability

SLM-specific use cases cluster around: domain-tuned knowledge workflows; operationally constrained environments (edge/IoT, high-volume real-time, intermittent connectivity); security scenarios requiring full training-data control; productivity tuned to internal style; and reasoning-heavy orchestration at low cost. The anti-patterns matter as much as the use cases: building an SLM when RAG over a general model already suffices; underestimating the data-curation and evaluation effort; using an SLM for broad creative reasoning; and — the exam's favorite misconception — SLMs are NOT inherently safer than LLMs and are not a misinformation silver bullet. Safety comes from data curation, tuning, and guardrails, none of which a small parameter count provides for free. Overfitting to narrow data, poor edge-case generalization, and rushed safety tuning are the named risks. [source, SLM research]

If you do customize an SLM, judge it with the SLM Success Scorecard — five metrics: task accuracy, latency, cost per 1,000 requests, safety incident rate, and drift. Note how naturally this slots into the router story: the scorecard's cost-per-1,000-requests and latency numbers are precisely the inputs a static or weighted routing rule consumes. The router is where model-choice economics goes to production. [source]

Check yourself

1. An invoice agent saves 4 minutes per invoice across 25,000 invoices/month at 80% adoption, labor rate $50/hour. Year-1 TCO is $600,000. What is the ROI?

Effective runs = 25,000 × 12 × 0.80 = 240,000. Hours saved = 240,000 × 4 ÷ 60 = 16,000. Annual benefit = 16,000 × $50 = $800,000. Net = $800,000 − $600,000 = $200,000. ROI = 200 ÷ 600 ≈ 33%. Option b nets but skips adoption (($1M − $600k) ÷ $600k); option c applies adoption but divides gross benefit by TCO; option d makes both errors. The correct arithmetic always applies adoption to volume and subtracts TCO before dividing.

2. An agent's initial one-time cost is $240,000. Steady state: annual benefit $720,000, annual run cost $120,000. What is the payback period?

Net monthly benefit = ($720,000 − $120,000) ÷ 12 = $50,000. Payback = $240,000 ÷ $50,000 = 4.8 months. Option a forgets the run cost ($240k ÷ $60k gross monthly); the other two are arithmetic noise. Payback divides the one-time cost by net monthly benefit — the formula's whole point is that run costs keep accruing while you recover the build.

3. A firm extends Microsoft 365 Copilot with connectors to its legacy knowledge estate. Twelve weeks in, the budget is blown — not on licenses or compute, but on deduplicating, re-permissioning, and restructuring content so retrieval works. Which TCO driver did they underestimate?

The one cell where the extend row breaks its medium-cost pattern is data quality & preparation: High. Grounding a platform on your content through connectors inherits your content's duplicates, stale versions, and broken permissions. The symptoms named — dedup, re-permissioning, restructuring — are the data-prep driver itemized.

4. A regional bank needs an agent for a standardized, undifferentiated HR process. AI maturity is low, no ML engineers are on staff, and leadership wants value this quarter with vendor support included. Recommendation?

Every trigger points to buy: time-to-value priority, standardized process, low AI maturity, vendor support wanted. Building would require the AI capability the bank lacks; extending still demands internal logic work and carries high data-prep cost; GPU hosting is the maximum-control option with no justification here.

5. You want 10% of production traffic to hit a newly deployed model, ramping gradually while you compare quality — no hard cutover. Which routing rule type?

Percentage-based traffic splitting for A/B tests and gradual rollouts is the weighted rule. Static maps attributes to models; fallback only fires on failure; version-based routes whole cohorts to stable versus experimental versions rather than splitting traffic by percentage.

6. Under which condition is investing in a customized SLM most clearly justified?

The justification sequence is prompt engineering → retrieval tuning → fine-tuning → custom model, and only demonstrated residual inaccuracy (or sovereignty / latency-cost-at-volume demands) clears the bar. Option a states the classic misconception — small models are not inherently safer; b and c are named anti-patterns (SLMs are wrong for broad creative reasoning, and underestimating curation/eval effort sinks these projects).

7. Admins have disabled money-based savings in Copilot Studio. How do you still build the ROI case for an agent?

The Savings capability degrades gracefully: when money-based savings are disabled, you track time saved and convert it to currency yourself — labor rate × hours, the same arithmetic as the ROI formula. Field research is a directional anchor at best; your own telemetry stays in the driver's seat.

8. Which ROI claim is most defensible in an executive business review?

ROI must be measurable, repeatable, outcome-aligned, and grounded in real usage analytics — measured pilot telemetry is the only option that satisfies all four. Studies and vendor materials are directional anchors; interviews are estimates. Own telemetry in the driver's seat, field research as a sanity check.

Apply it: Litware's returns operation

Decide each before opening the reveal. Scenario A is a full ROI calculation — do the arithmetic on paper first; the reveal checks your numbers, not your intuition.

A. Litware (distribution, 6,000 employees) proposes a returns-processing agent. Measured pilot data: 2.5 minutes saved per return, 40,000 returns per month, expected adoption 65%, loaded labor rate $38/hour. Year-1 TCO is $250,000, of which $180,000 is one-time build cost; steady-state run cost is $70,000/year. Compute: annual benefit, net benefit, ROI%, and payback months.

Your numbers?

Effective runs/year = 40,000 × 12 × 0.65 = 312,000 (adoption multiplies volume). Hours saved = 312,000 × 2.5 ÷ 60 = 13,000 hours. Annual benefit = 13,000 × $38 = $494,000. Net vs year-1 TCO = $494,000 − $250,000 = $244,000. ROI = 244 ÷ 250 ≈ 98%. Payback: net monthly benefit at steady state = ($494,000 − $70,000) ÷ 12 ≈ $35,333; $180,000 ÷ $35,333 ≈ 5.1 months. Sensitivity check: at a conservative 50% adoption, benefit falls to $380,000 — net still positive ($130k, ROI ~52%), so the case is robust. Present exactly that band on the executive one-slide. [source]

B. The same ROI review surfaces a second initiative: a quoting agent for Litware's configure-price-quote flow. Quoting is how Litware wins deals — speed and accuracy there are genuine market differentiation. The flow touches a proprietary pricing engine no vendor supports, pricing data is contractually sensitive, and Litware has a strong data science team. A vendor demo, an extend-Copilot proposal, and a custom Foundry build are on the table. Choose, then sketch the model-routing strategy for your choice.

Your call?

Build — the triggers line up exactly: competitive differentiation is the point, prebuilt can't reach the proprietary engine, data sensitivity demands full control, and the AI/ML capability exists. Expect the build column's costs: high infrastructure, development, expertise, and operations — and don't let data prep sneak up on you even here. Router design: register the deployed models in Foundry's model router and define the strategy as hybrid cost/performance. Static rules route simple intents (quote-status lookups, FAQ) to an SLM and complex pricing reasoning to the large model; a fallback rule protects the quoting flow during peak season by failing over to a backup deployment; weighted routing (10% → ramp) governs every future model upgrade; version-based routing keeps the canary cohort on experimental versions while production stays on latest stable. Monitor latency, cost, accuracy, and usage distribution — then tune. [source, source]

C. For the quoting build, an engineer proposes fine-tuning an SLM to classify inbound quote requests by intent — 2 million classifications per day — arguing "a small model will also be safer, so compliance will sign off faster." Evaluate the proposal.

Your call?

The economics may well justify it; the safety argument does not. Intent classification at 2M requests/day is a legitimate SLM case: a narrow, well-bounded task where latency and cost per request at volume dominate, and a customized SLM can undercut a frontier model's cost per 1,000 requests by a wide margin. But first run the checklist — does prompt engineering or retrieval tuning over a catalog model already hit the accuracy bar? Fine-tune only with demonstrated residual inaccuracy. And kill the misconception in the design doc: SLMs are not inherently safer and don't reduce misinformation by default — compliance sign-off still requires the data curation, safety tuning, and guardrail evidence any model would. Judge the result with the SLM Success Scorecard: task accuracy, latency, cost per 1,000 requests, safety incident rate, drift — then wire it in behind the router with a static rule (task = classification → SLM) and a fallback to the larger model. [source]
